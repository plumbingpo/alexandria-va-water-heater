No hot water from a gas heater usually means one thing: the burner is not firing, and something told it not to. Gas heaters are built around safety. The control valve will not open unless a thermocouple or flame sensor proves there is a flame, and many models lock out if the flame arrestor screen at the bottom gets clogged or the heater senses spilled exhaust. So the real question is which safety did its job, and why. Across Alexandria, VA, the answer is most often a worn thermocouple, a dirty pilot or burner, a lint-choked air intake, a failing gas valve, or a vent that is no longer drafting properly.

Every gas water heater call starts with safety and then moves to testing. If you ever smell gas, leave the home first, call the gas utility from outside, and do not operate switches or try to relight anything. Once the area is safe, our Alexandria technicians work through the heater systematically: millivolt readings on the thermocouple, a close look at the pilot and main burner flames, gas pressure at the control valve, a draft test to make sure exhaust is not spilling back, and a CO check around the appliance. Scorch marks at the burner access, rust flakes on the burner, and a lazy yellow flame each point to a different problem, and we chase down the right one.

The fix is usually straightforward once the cause is known. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. When replacement is the smarter move, we install new gas heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, with proper atmospheric, power vent, or direct vent venting, a drip leg on the gas supply, and the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code calls for. Warning Signs to Watch For

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Water pooling around the base of the heater
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • A silent heater with no flame and no hot water
  • Popping, rumbling, or crackling sounds as the burner heats
  • A faint gas or rotten-egg smell near the heater (leave and call the gas utility)
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit
  • Less hot water than the tank should deliver
  • Signs of backdrafting, such as soot, condensation, or an exhaust smell at the vent

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Start with the hazards. Every gas water heater involves fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust that contains carbon monoxide. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and replacing them one by one is slow and costly.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Keep the air intake at the bottom of the heater free of lint and dust
  • Make sure your home has CO alarms that work, especially with gas appliances
  • Check that the vent connector is secure and rising toward the chimney or flue
  • Give the heater breathing room and keep flammables well away from it
  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
  • Keep the thermostat around 120 degrees to save energy and reduce scalding risk
